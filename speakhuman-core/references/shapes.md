# Slop shapes

Twelve shapes cover most semantic slop. Each is a way a sentence behaves, so a new example on any topic belongs to one of them even when no word list has seen it. The judge pass classifies sentences against these shapes. Examples span unrelated subjects on purpose, so the move is what you learn and the topic does not matter.

## S1. Image as claim

A plain subject is declared to be a vivid object, force, place or process, and the image replaces the evidence. Test: delete the image. Is there a claim left, with a number, name or mechanism behind it?

- A lease is a leash with paperwork.
- The tide of public opinion never stops turning.
- Your inbox is a river that never runs dry.
- Her second novel was a house built on the first one's foundation.

Not S1: a one-clause image that carries a specific, checkable point ("The valve acts as a check on backflow: it opens at 5 psi and shuts below it").

## S2. Stinger closer

A short sentence at the end of a paragraph or section that moralizes, restates, or reaches for significance. Test: delete it. Is any fact lost?

- Good bridges outlast the people who build them.
- That is the cost of cutting corners.
- The map is not the territory, and the territory knows it.
- In the end, the work speaks for itself.

Not S2: a short last sentence that adds a fact, a number, a consequence or the next step ("The permit lapsed on May 4").

## S3. Foil contrast

The point is made by setting up a straw view and knocking it down, or by naming what the thing is not. Includes "X is not Y, it is Z", "most people treat X as A; X is B", "less about X, more about Y", "we do not X, we Y", "the question is not whether but who", and the quiet form "People think X. It is Y."

- Fermentation isn't cooking, it's patience.
- Most commuters treat the train as dead time. The train is a second office.
- The debate is less about taxes and more about trust.
- Cities do not have a traffic problem. They have a land-use problem.

## S3b. Parallel antithesis

Two short sentences mirrored so the second flips or caps the first.

- The launch was loud. The adoption was quiet.
- Plans are cheap. Execution is rare.
- The sprint ended. The backlog did not.

## S4. Portable truism

A general statement with no number, name or specific, true of almost anything, so it could be moved into an article on another subject unchanged. Test: swap the topic. Does the sentence still read fine?

- Details matter.
- Patience is rewarded.
- Every great team starts with trust.
- Quality is never an accident.

## S5. Announced significance

The sentence tells the reader that something matters, surprises, or changes everything, instead of showing it.

- This is where it gets interesting.
- That changes everything.
- The implications are significant.
- Here is why that matters.

## S6. Personified abstraction

An abstraction or object does a human verb, or a narrator comments from above.

- The market punishes hesitation.
- The data wants to be heard.
- Old buildings remember.
- History has a way of repeating itself.

## S7. Padded or tidy triad

A list of three (or a balanced pair) chosen for rhythm, where the third item adds nothing or the items are abstract nouns.

- It was fast, flexible and future-proof.
- She brought curiosity, courage and craft.
- Plan, build, learn.

## S8. Rhetorical setup

A question asked only to be answered at once, or a one-word reveal.

- So what is the lesson?
- The result? Fewer complaints.
- Why does this matter? Because trust compounds.

## S9. Restated conclusion or announced argument

The paragraph or section repeats what was already said in new words, or announces what it is about to argue.

- In short, the evidence points one way.
- This article will argue that the schedule is the problem.
- All of this leads to a simple conclusion.

## S10. Stock adage or attributed cliche

A saying everyone has heard, used as if it were an insight.

- Culture eats strategy for breakfast.
- You miss every shot you do not take.
- People forget what you said but remember how you made them feel.
- A rising tide lifts all boats.

## S11. Unnecessary inference

A sentence draws the conclusion the reader already gets from the fact before it. The tell is explanatory surplus: it explains the obvious consequence and adds nothing the reader could not supply. Test: delete it. Does the reader lose anything they could not infer?

- Complaints fell 42 percent. That means customers were having a better experience.
- The build finished nine days early. Finishing early gave the team more time.
- Conversion rose from 4 to 7 percent. The campaign was clearly more effective.

Not S11: an inference that adds a step the reader would not take alone ("Complaints fell 42 percent, so we cut the overtime budget in half").

## S12. Unsupported causal glue

A clause asserts that one thing caused or enabled another, with no evidence of the link or its size. Common forms: ", enabling Y", ", helping Y", ", allowing Y", ", which means Y", ", driving Y".

- The team centralized the assets, enabling marketers to move faster.
- We simplified the form, helping customers finish with less friction.
- The new dashboard surfaces trends, driving better decisions.

Ask: did it? By how much? Was the first thing the cause? Not S12: a cause the reader can verify inside the sentence ("The valve closed, stopping the flow").

## How to apply them

Read each sentence with its paragraph and its position in the piece. Openers draw S4, S5 and S9 (a hook that promises meaning instead of stating a fact). Middle paragraphs draw S1, S3 and S6 (an image or a foil standing in for evidence). Closers draw S2, S4 and S9.

Concrete facts weigh against a finding but do not clear a sentence by themselves. A number, a date or a name can sit inside a cliche ("Salesforce is the beating heart of our operation, serving 17 teams", "On March 4, everything changed", "Jane Smith saw what nobody else could see"). Ask what the sentence is doing: if the fact carries the claim, the sentence is clean; if an image, a flourish or an announcement carries the claim and the fact is decoration, flag it.

Use the swap test for S4 and for generic conclusions only. A sentence that passes it can still be slop, and one that fails it can be exactly right ("The first attempt failed" fits any article and may be the correct sentence in context). Portability is evidence of generality, not of slop in general.

A single use of a device (one contrast, one metaphor, one rhetorical question, one triad) is normally a choice. Flag a device when it repeats in the piece, stands in for evidence, or is generic. Say so in the reason.
